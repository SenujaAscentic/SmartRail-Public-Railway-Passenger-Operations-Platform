# Session driver-2026-10-06T05-10-56-295Z

Topic: Describe your normal shift and your normal week right now: hour by hour what you do, which instructions, papers, radios or devices you use, who you talk to, what goes wrong and how often.

**moderator**

What time do you officially start your shift, and what is the very first task you perform when you arrive?

---

**driver**

My shift usually starts at 04:30 at the Maradana running shed. The very first thing I do is sign into the logbook and collect my "driver’s pack"—that’s the paper circulars for the day. These sheets tell me about any temporary speed restrictions, track maintenance, or signal failures along the Main Line.

I don’t touch a phone. I check my watch against the station clock, grab my radio, and head straight to the locomotive to perform a physical walk-around. I’m checking the brake pipe pressure, the headlights, and the sanders. If the radio isn't crisp, I report it to the shed foreman immediately. I’ve been doing this for 18 years; I trust the steel and the signals, not a screen. Once I’m in the cab, my focus is entirely on the line ahead. If the controller tells me to move, I move. If there’s a discrepancy between my paper notes and the signal aspect, I stop and wait for a verbal confirmation over the radio. Safety isn't an app.

**moderator**

Once you are in the cab and have received the instruction to move, what is the sequence of tasks you perform during the first hour of your journey, and which specific communication do you have with the signalman or controller during this time?

---

**driver**

Once I’m in the cab, the first hour is about routine and vigilance. After the "Right Away" signal—either from the guard or the platform staff—I check my gauges again. As I pull out of Maradana or Fort, my eyes are locked on the signal aspects. That is the only language that matters.

I don’t talk to the controller unless I have to. If the signals are green, I keep moving. If I’m held at a red signal, I use the radio to call the signalman to confirm the route ahead. It’s always, "Kasun, driver of [Train Number], standing at signal [Number]." I wait for their verbal authority before I move an inch.

I’m constantly checking the paper circulars against the track-side markers for temporary speed restrictions—say, a 20 km/h limit near a bridge or a work site. I don't need a screen to tell me where I am. I know every curve, every gradient, and every tunnel on the Main Line by heart. My hands are on the throttle and the brake. If the signalman has a change of plan, they’ll radio me. Until then, I stay focused on the line.

**moderator**

When you complete your final trip and return the locomotive to the shed, what specific logs or paperwork do you need to sign or submit before you officially clock out?

---

**driver**

When I pull back into the shed, the shift isn’t over until the paperwork matches the reality of the day. First, I fill out the official logbook. I record my arrival time, the engine number, and the mileage. If there were any delays—and there usually are, whether it’s a signal failure near Gampaha or a passenger emergency—I note the exact time and reason.

I also have to sign off on the "Defect Book." If the locomotive felt sluggish, if the radio was cutting out in the tunnels, or if a headlight was flickering, I write it down. The shed foreman needs to see that before the next driver takes this engine out.

Then, I hand back my radio and the remains of my paper circulars. If I had to take a written instruction from the controller mid-journey, that gets stapled to my report. I don’t leave until the foreman acknowledges the log and signs off on my departure. Once that’s done, I hand in my key, clock out, and that’s it. My accountability ends there. I don't take the work home with me.

**moderator**

How does your typical workday change on the weekends compared to your weekday shifts?

---

**driver**

Weekends are a different beast entirely. On weekdays, I’m usually hauling commuters from Veyangoda or Panadura; it’s a tight, fast-paced schedule where every minute counts. The platforms are packed, and the pressure to keep the timetable is immense because if I’m two minutes late, the whole line suffers.

On Saturdays and Sundays, the schedule changes. We run fewer commuter services and more intercity trains, especially on the Main Line towards Kandy. The passengers change—it’s families and tourists rather than office workers. The pace is technically slower, but the trains are heavier and often overcrowded with people heading out for the weekend.

The biggest difference is the maintenance. Track work is almost always scheduled for the weekends because there’s less traffic. I get more "caution" orders in my paper pack for speed restrictions where crews are working on the tracks. I have to be even more alert for the red flags or hand signals from the track gangs. It’s quieter at the shed, but the responsibility is exactly the same. The line doesn't care if it's a Sunday; the signals don't change their meaning.

MODERATOR: What is the specific issue that goes wrong most often in a typical week, how often does it happen, and what is your exact process for dealing with it?

---

**driver**

The most frequent issue is signal failure or communication breakdown due to power issues or old equipment. It happens at least two or three times a week, especially when the weather turns during the monsoon.

When I face a signal failure—usually a "dead" signal or one stuck at red—my process is rigid. I immediately bring the train to a full stop. I do not guess. I pick up the radio and contact the nearest signalman. I give my train number, my exact location, and the signal identification number. 

I wait for the signalman to issue a "Caution Order" or "Paper Authority" to proceed past the danger signal. If we are in a dead spot, like a tunnel or a deep cutting where the radio is patchy, I have to wait until I have a clear signal to call, or use the emergency trackside telephone if there is one nearby. 

I don't move until I have that verbal or written clearance. I then record the time of the delay and the signal number in my logbook. It’s frustrating, but I’m not risking lives or my license just to make up for a faulty circuit.
