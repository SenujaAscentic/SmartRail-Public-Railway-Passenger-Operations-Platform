# Session station-manager-2026-10-06T05-11-58-385Z

Topic: Describe your normal day and your normal week at the station right now: hour by hour what you and your staff do, which boards, radios, phones or systems you use, who you talk to, what goes wrong and how often.

**moderator**

What is the very first thing you do when you arrive for your shift, at what time does this happen, and what physical or digital tools do you use to get that process started?

---

**station-manager**

I arrive at 06:00 sharp. The first thing I do is walk the floor to check the platform boards against the official printed timetable we keep in the office. I don’t trust the digital displays until I’ve verified them with my own eyes. 

I head straight to the station master’s office to check the logbook. I need to see what happened during the night shift—any signal failures or technical hitches that might bite us when the morning rush from places like Panadura hits at 07:00. I check my radio to ensure it’s tuned to the control frequency for Colombo Fort. 

I also grab my personal mobile to check the RDMNS app. It’s unofficial, but it tells me what the passengers are saying before the control room tells me what’s actually happening. If the app shows a train is stuck at Maradana but my board says it’s on time, I know I’m in for a long, difficult morning. I rely on that paper timetable, my radio, and my staff on the platforms to tell me the truth.

---

**moderator**

Between 07:00 and 09:00, when the morning rush is at its peak, what specific information do you receive from your staff on the platforms, and how do they relay that information to you?

---

**station-manager**

During the 07:00–09:00 peak, my platform staff are my eyes and ears. They don't have fancy tablets; they use handheld radios and their own voices. They report back to me on the platform edge—specifically, how far back the crowd is pushing. If the 07:30 from Kalutara is delayed, the platform gets dangerous within ten minutes. My staff will radio me: "Manager, Platform 2 is at capacity, we need to hold entry at the barriers."

They also flag 'false' information. If a display shows a train is arriving but they see the track is empty, they radio the office immediately. I then have to decide whether to manually override the PA system to announce the delay before the passengers start shouting at my staff. 

It’s chaotic. I’m constantly balancing the radio chatter with the physical reality of hundreds of commuters trying to board. If I don't get that update from the platform staff before the passengers start asking, I’ve already lost control of the floor. My staff are the only thing preventing a stampede when the boards fail us.

---

**moderator**

At the end of your shift, how do you formally hand over the station status to the incoming manager, and what specific documents or logs are used during this process?

---

**station-manager**

My shift ends at 14:00, and the handover is done in the station master’s office. It’s a physical, face-to-face process—there is no digital "syncing" here. 

I sit down with the incoming manager and we go through the station logbook. This is a heavy, bound ledger where we record every platform alteration, passenger incident, or equipment failure that occurred during my eight hours. If a lift was out of service or if we had a dispute at the ticket counter, it is written in ink. 

I hand over my radio, ensuring the battery is swapped, and pass on any verbal warnings about the state of the infrastructure. I’ll tell them, "The display on Platform 3 is flickering again; ignore it and use the megaphone." 

We don’t have a formal "handover document" beyond that logbook. It’s all about context—telling them which train drivers were running behind, which platforms were overcrowded, and if there’s any lingering tension from the morning rush. I sign off, they sign in, and I leave the station knowing that if the system breaks at 18:00 on a Friday, it’s their problem to manage, not mine.

**moderator**

How does your workflow or the volume of tasks you manage change on a Saturday or Sunday compared to a typical weekday?

---

**station-manager**

Weekends are a different breed of chaos. During the week, it’s all about managing the sheer volume of commuters from Gampaha or Panadura—it’s a numbers game, focused on safety and keeping the lines moving. On Saturdays and Sundays, the volume drops, but the nature of the passengers changes entirely.

We get more tourists heading to the hill country or the south coast, and they are completely unfamiliar with our system. On a weekday, if a train is delayed, the regulars know exactly what’s happening. On a weekend, if the display shows a delay, I’m suddenly swamped by confused travelers asking me to explain the entire network.

I have fewer staff on duty, which makes simple things like a broken lift much harder to manage. If a lift fails on a Sunday, I don’t have the extra bodies to assist passengers with luggage, so I’m often stuck handling that myself. The pressure isn't about preventing a stampede; it’s about managing the constant stream of questions. The logbook entries are shorter, but the frustration of dealing with stale, inaccurate information displays remains exactly the same. It’s quieter, but just as unpredictable.

---

**moderator**

MODERATOR: What is the single most common problem that causes a disruption during your week, how often does it occur, and what are the specific steps you take to resolve it?

---

**station-manager**

The most common disruption is a sudden, unannounced platform change for a Main Line express. It happens at least three or four times a week. The control room at Colombo Fort decides to switch a train to a different platform to bypass congestion, but they don't update the electronic displays—or worse, they update them too late.

When that happens, I don't wait for a signal. I see the crowd on Platform 1 starting to surge toward a train that is actually pulling into Platform 3. I immediately grab the microphone for the PA system and override the automated announcements. I have to shout over the noise in three languages—Sinhala, Tamil, and English—to redirect the flow. 

Simultaneously, I radio my staff on the platforms to physically block the stairs or the platform edge. It is a high-pressure scramble. If we don’t catch it in the first sixty seconds, we end up with hundreds of people trying to cross tracks or push through narrow stairwells. It’s a constant battle against a system that thinks the station is a digital map, while I’m the one dealing with the actual, moving bodies on the floor.
