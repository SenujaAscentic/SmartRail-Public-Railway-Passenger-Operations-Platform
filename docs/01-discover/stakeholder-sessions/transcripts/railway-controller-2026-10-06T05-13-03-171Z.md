# Session railway-controller-2026-10-06T05-13-03-171Z

Topic: Describe your normal shift and your normal week in the control room right now: hour by hour what you monitor and decide, which logs, radios, phones or screens you use, who you talk to, what goes wrong and how often.

**moderator**

When you arrive for your shift, what is the very first thing you do, and who do you talk to to get a handover of the current state of the network?

---

**railway-controller**

I clock in at 05:45 for the early shift. The first thing I do is scan the logbook on the desk—it’s the only record that doesn't lie. I don't rely on the digital displays initially; they’re often lagging behind reality. 

I head straight to the outgoing controller. I don't want pleasantries; I want the state of play. We go through the "hot" list: which signals are playing up between Fort and Maradana, which intercity trains are running short-formed, and any speed restrictions due to track work or weather. 

I check the radio logs and the handwritten notes from the night shift regarding the Coastal Line. If a train is stuck or an engine failed near Panadura, I need to know exactly where it is and if the relief locomotive has been dispatched. I talk to the outgoing shift lead for five minutes, confirm the current positions of the first morning express trains, and then I take the desk. The responsibility is mine from that second. If the information isn't in the log or the radio feed, it doesn't exist.

---

**moderator**

Once you take the desk at 05:45, what is the first specific decision you have to make, and which screen or physical board do you look at to make that call?

---

**railway-controller**

The first decision is almost always platform allocation for the incoming commuter rush at Colombo Fort. The 06:05 from Gampaha is usually approaching, and I need to ensure the platform is clear of any lingering night-mail or stabled rolling stock.

I look at the physical track diagram board on the wall—the one with the indicator lights for section occupancy. The digital screens often show a train as "arrived" before it has physically cleared the signal section, which is a recipe for a collision or a signal failure. I verify the train's position by cross-referencing that board with the radio report I just received from the signalman at Maradana.

If the board shows a section is occupied but the radio is silent, I have to decide: do I hold the incoming train at the outer home signal or push it into a secondary platform, potentially blocking the path for the 06:15 Northern Line departure? I make that call based on the board, not the computer. Speed is vital, but guessing is not an option. If I’m wrong, the whole morning schedule for the Main Line collapses before 07:00.

**moderator**

When your shift comes to an end, what is the specific process for handing over the state of the network to the next controller, and which documents or logs do you physically hand over to them?

---

**railway-controller**

My shift ends at 14:00. The handover is a ritual of accountability. I don’t leave until the relief controller is sitting in the chair and has physically touched the logbook.

I hand over the "Daily Occurrence Book." This is the non-negotiable record. Every delay over ten minutes, every signal failure, and every unscheduled stop is written there in pen. If it isn’t in the book, it didn't happen. I also hand over the shift report sheet, which summarizes the status of the long-distance trains—the ones heading up to Kandy or down to Matara.

I walk them through the "live" issues: which signals are intermittent, which drivers reported track defects, and the status of any relief engines currently out on the line. I point to the wall diagram to show them exactly where the current bottlenecks are. I tell them, "The 13:45 from Fort is running fifteen minutes late due to a passenger alarm near Kelaniya; keep an eye on the platform capacity." 

Once they acknowledge the state of the network and sign the logbook, my responsibility ends. Until then, I am the one on the hook.

**moderator**

How does the volume or nature of the traffic you monitor on a weekend differ from a standard weekday, and are there specific tasks or checks you perform on those days that you don't do during the week?

---

**railway-controller**

Weekends are a different beast. On weekdays, the pressure is about throughput—moving thousands of commuters in and out of Fort as fast as possible. On weekends, the nature of the traffic shifts to long-distance leisure travel and tourists heading to Kandy or the South Coast.

The volume of commuter trains drops, but the trains themselves are often longer and packed with families. I have to be more vigilant about dwelling times at platforms; people take longer to board with luggage, which causes "knock-on" delays that aren't as predictable as the morning rush.

The specific check I perform is the "Rolling Stock Audit." I have to verify that the 1st and 2nd class carriages are actually attached to the intercity trains as scheduled. If a unit was pulled for maintenance on Friday night and not replaced, I have to re-allocate platform space or adjust departure priorities to prevent a riot on the platform. I spend more time on the radio with station masters at minor stops, ensuring they aren't overwhelmed by weekend crowds. It’s less about raw speed and more about managing the chaos of irregular travel patterns.

---

**moderator**

In a typical week, what is the single most common type of operational issue that goes wrong, how often does it occur, and what are the specific steps you take to resolve it?

---

**railway-controller**

The most frequent operational issue is a "stalled" commuter train due to a passenger alarm or a door obstruction, usually on the Coastal Line during the peak hours. It happens at least three or four times a week. 

When the alarm sounds, the train stops immediately. The driver radios me. I don’t wait for the digital system to update; I immediately instruct the station master at the nearest stop to send a guard or a technician to the carriage. 

My priority is the "section clearance." If the train is stuck between stations, I have to stop all following traffic to prevent a pile-up. I use the wall diagram to freeze the block sections. Once the driver confirms the issue—usually a minor mechanical fault or a passenger pulling the chain—I decide whether to authorize a manual bypass or dispatch a relief engine. If it’s a delay over fifteen minutes, I call the station masters at the next three major stops to inform them of the knock-on effect. It’s a constant battle to stop a minor delay from cascading into a total line blockage.
