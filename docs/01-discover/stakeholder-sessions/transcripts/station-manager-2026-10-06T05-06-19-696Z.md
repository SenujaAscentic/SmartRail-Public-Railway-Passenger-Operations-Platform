Topic: Describe your normal day and your normal week at the station right now: hour by hour what you and your staff do, which boards, radios, phones or systems you use, who you talk to, what goes wrong and how often.

**moderator**

Station-manager, could you walk me through your morning peak-hour routine and identify which specific system or communication channel you rely on most when a train is delayed or a platform becomes dangerously overcrowded?

---

**station-manager**

My morning peak is chaos, plain and simple. Between 07:00 and 09:00, the platforms are packed with commuters heading into Colombo Fort. 

When a train is delayed, I don't have a single "go-to" channel. I have my radio, my mobile, and the station controller’s desk. My biggest frustration is that I often hear the bad news from angry passengers who have checked the RDMNS app before my own screens have updated. That makes my staff look incompetent.

If a platform hits capacity—say, a delayed Main Line train causes a surge—I need to be able to close access or redirect flow immediately. Currently, I’m waiting on the central controller to push updates to the boards. That’s too slow. I need local override authority to post "Platform Full" or "Use Footbridge" notices on my screens instantly.

And what happens at 18:00 on a Friday when the system goes down? If the network drops, my boards go blank or, worse, show stale, incorrect data. That is a safety hazard. I need those displays to show a "Last Updated" timestamp or a "Check with Staff" message if they lose connection. We cannot have passengers standing on the wrong platform based on ghosts.

**station-manager**

Listen, I don't need a bureaucratic "verification process" that involves calling a desk at Fort during a crisis. When a platform is at capacity, seconds matter. I need a "Local Emergency Mode" on my terminal.

If I trigger a "Platform Full" alert, I should be able to select from a pre-approved list of tactical messages—like "Directing traffic to Platform 3" or "Temporary hold on entry." The system should automatically send an instant, silent acknowledgment to the controller’s screen so they *see* that I’ve taken control of the display. 

The protocol is simple: I am accountable for my station. If I change a board, my staff is already on the ground to manage the flow. The current system treats me like a spectator. 

And don't forget my Friday 18:00 scenario: if the network is down, the system *must* allow me to manually type a message to the screens via a local server. If I can't inform my passengers that the 18:15 is delayed because the central server is offline, then the entire platform becomes a safety liability. I need the tools to control my own floor.

**moderator**

MODERATOR: STATION-MANAGER: You mentioned that you and your staff are the ones on the ground managing the flow when you trigger these local alerts; how do you currently coordinate that physical crowd control with your staff, and what specific gaps exist in your communication loop that would prevent them from being caught off guard when you change a display board?

---

**station-manager**

Right now, coordination is mostly done by shouting, hand signals, and handheld radios. It’s archaic. My staff are spread across platforms, the ticket office, and the footbridge. If I change a board, I have to radio each team leader individually to tell them the plan. In the noise of a peak-hour crowd, they don't always hear me, or the radio signal dies in the tunnels.

The gap is **synchronization**. When I hit "Platform Full" on my terminal, my staff should get an instant notification on their own handheld devices. They shouldn't be finding out by looking at the board; they should know *before* the board changes so they are already in position to manage the crowd.

The brief talks about "notifications," but it doesn't mention internal operational messaging. I need a way to push a "crowd control plan" to my team simultaneously—not just a status update, but an instruction. If I’m moving people to the footbridge, the team there needs to be ready for the surge before it hits them. Currently, we are reacting to the crowd. I need us to be managing it.

---

**station-manager**

If the network drops at 18:00 on a Friday, I cannot have my staff staring at dead screens or spinning wheels. If the Wi-Fi or cellular signal dies, those devices must revert to a local mesh or peer-to-peer mode—they need to talk to each other and my terminal directly, without needing to reach a central server in Colombo.

But even with a local mesh, technology fails. My primary fallback is always human: the system must have a "broadcast to all" feature that works as long as the device is powered. If the digital link is severed, the devices must vibrate and flash a pre-set "Emergency Protocol" alert, forcing my staff to immediately switch to their physical roles—holding lines, blocking stairs, or guiding passengers to the footbridge—based on our standard, pre-briefed disruption drills.

The brief ignores that a station is a physical space, not just a data node. If the tech goes dark, the training takes over. My requirement is that the device must give a clear "Offline - Manual Control" warning immediately, so my staff knows the data is stale and they need to rely on their eyes and my voice.

**moderator**

MODERATOR: STATION-MANAGER: You’ve highlighted the critical need for local control and staff synchronization during a crisis, but let's talk about the "aftermath"—when a surge finally subsides, how is the "all-clear" communicated back to the central controller and the passengers to ensure that the station doesn't remain in a restricted, inefficient state longer than necessary?

---

**station-manager**

The "all-clear" is just as critical as the emergency. Right now, it’s a guessing game. The central controller often doesn't know the surge has passed, so they keep holding trains or skipping stops, which creates a secondary delay for no reason.

When I am ready to return to normal, I need a "Resume Normal Operations" button on my terminal. This should do three things simultaneously: 
1. Revert the displays to the live schedule. 
2. Send a "Clear" notification to my staff’s devices so they know they can stop the manual crowd-management protocols. 
3. Send an automated, high-priority status update to the central controller’s system. 

The brief is completely silent on **state synchronization**. It treats the station as a passive recipient of data, not an active participant in the network. If I don't have a clean way to signal that the station is fluid again, we end up with "ghost" restrictions—where the system thinks we are still in crisis mode at 19:30, even though the platform is empty. 

I need the system to confirm that the controller has received my "all-clear" signal. If they don't acknowledge it, I need a visual flag on my screen so I know to pick up the phone. We cannot leave the network in a state of flux based on assumptions.
